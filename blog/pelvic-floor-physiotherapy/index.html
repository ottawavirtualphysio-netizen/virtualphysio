Pelvic Floor Physiotherapy: Symptoms, Treatment and What to Expect

Bladder leaks, pelvic pain and heaviness are common, but they aren't something you just have to live with. Pelvic floor physiotherapy is a well-established, effective treatment for many of these problems.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished July 10, 2026 · Updated November 25, 20263 min read

What is the pelvic floor?

The pelvic floor is a group of muscles that form a sling at the base of your pelvis. These muscles support the bladder, bowel and uterus, help control when you pee and poo, contribute to sexual function and work with your deep core muscles and breathing.

Like any muscles, they can be weak, overly tight or poorly coordinated. Pregnancy, childbirth, menopause, chronic coughing, heavy lifting, constipation and surgery can all affect them.

Signs your pelvic floor may need help

  • Leaking urine when you cough, sneeze, laugh, jump or lift
  • Sudden, strong urges to pee, or needing to go very often
  • A feeling of heaviness, dragging or bulging in the vagina
  • Difficulty fully emptying your bladder or bowel
  • Pain during sex, tampon use or pelvic exams
  • Pelvic, tailbone or low back pain that hasn't improved with other treatment
  • Leaking gas or stool

These problems can affect people of any gender. Men may have pelvic floor issues after prostate surgery or with chronic pelvic pain.

"Just do Kegels"? Not always

Pelvic floor strengthening is very effective for many people with stress leakage. But some people have pelvic floor muscles that are too tense rather than weak, and doing more squeezing can make pain worse. Others squeeze the wrong muscles without realizing it. A proper assessment makes sure you're doing the right exercise, correctly, for your situation.

What happens at an appointment?

Your first visit starts with a detailed, private conversation about your symptoms, bladder and bowel habits, pregnancies and births, and goals. Your physiotherapist will then explain the assessment options. These may include looking at your posture, breathing, abdominal wall and hips, and, with your consent, an internal assessment of the pelvic floor muscles.

You are always in control. You can ask questions at any point, decline any part of the assessment and stop at any time. Treatment can still go ahead without an internal exam.

Treatment may include

  • Pelvic floor muscle training to strengthen, or relaxation training to release tension
  • Coordinating the pelvic floor with breathing and core muscles
  • Bladder training and practical habit changes
  • Advice on fluids, constipation and safe lifting
  • A gradual plan to return to running, lifting or sport
  • Hands-on techniques for pain or scar tissue

Can pelvic floor physiotherapy be done virtually?

A lot of it can. Education, breathing and coordination training, bladder retraining, core exercise and return-to-activity planning all work well over video. Virtual care is also a comfortable first step if you feel nervous about an appointment. If a hands-on assessment would be helpful, your physiotherapist will discuss an in-person option.

During pregnancy and after birth

Pelvic floor physiotherapy can help you prepare for birth, manage pelvic girdle pain during pregnancy and recover afterwards. Many people book a postpartum check around six weeks after birth. Read more in our guide to postpartum core recovery and diastasis recti.

See your doctor promptly for blood in your urine, unexplained bleeding, a fever with pelvic pain, or sudden loss of bladder or bowel control.

Frequently asked questions

Is leaking after having a baby normal?

It's common, but it isn't something you have to accept. Pelvic floor physiotherapy is a recommended first-line treatment for stress urinary incontinence.

Do I have to have an internal exam?

No. An internal assessment can give useful information, but it's always optional and only done with your consent. Treatment can still be effective without it.

How long does pelvic floor training take to work?

Many people notice improvement in 6 to 12 weeks of consistent training. Muscle strength continues to improve for several months.

This article is general information and isn't a substitute for an individual assessment. If you're unsure about your symptoms, speak to a physiotherapist or your doctor.

Sports Injury Prevention: 8 Physio Tips for Weekend Athletes

Hockey in winter, running and soccer in summer, pickleball year-round: Ottawa keeps weekend athletes busy. Most sports injuries aren't bad luck. They come from doing too much, too soon, with too little preparation.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished July 10, 2026 · Updated November 25, 20263 min read

The most common weekend-athlete injuries

  • Ankle sprains from cutting and landing sports like soccer, basketball and pickleball
  • Hamstring and calf strains from sprinting without enough preparation
  • Knee pain, including patellofemoral pain and ligament sprains
  • Achilles and patellar tendon pain from sudden jumps in running or jumping volume
  • Shoulder and elbow overuse in racquet and throwing sports
  • Low back strain from golf, hockey and lifting

1. Warm up with movement, not just stretching

A good warm-up raises your heart rate, moves joints through the range your sport needs and gets you ready for speed. Spend 8 to 10 minutes on light jogging, skipping or cycling, then dynamic movements like leg swings, lunges with a twist and arm circles, then a few short accelerations. In research reviews, structured warm-up programs such as FIFA 11+ have been linked to fewer injuries in soccer players (see references).

2. Build up gradually

Many injuries happen when activity jumps suddenly: the first long run of spring, a tournament weekend, or going from no hockey to three games a week. Increase training time or distance gradually, and plan a lighter week every few weeks.

3. Strength train twice a week

Strong muscles and tendons tolerate load better, which is why strength work is a common part of injury-prevention programs. Two short sessions a week focused on the legs, hips, core and shoulders is a practical place to start. Good basics include:

  • Squats or sit-to-stands
  • Split squats or lunges
  • Hip bridges and side planks
  • Calf raises, slowly lowering on one leg
  • Nordic hamstring curls (start with a small range)
  • Rows and band exercises for the shoulders

4. Train your balance and landing

Balance and landing drills are especially important if you've sprained an ankle before, since a previous sprain raises the chance of spraining it again. Practice standing on one leg, then add reaching, catching or eyes-closed work. Practice landing softly from small hops with knees tracking over your toes.

5. Respect recovery

Sleep, food and rest days are when your body adapts to training. Plan at least one or two rest or easy days a week.

6. Check your equipment

Replace running shoes when the cushioning is worn out. Make sure skates, helmets and racquets fit properly. For racquet sports, grip size and string tension can affect elbow pain.

7. Don't ignore early warning signs

Tendon and overuse problems usually whisper before they shout. Stiffness that warms up and goes away during exercise but is worse the next morning is a common early sign. Adjusting your training then is much easier than recovering from a full flare-up.

8. Get a movement screen

A physiotherapist can look at your strength, mobility, balance and sport-specific movements and find weak links before they turn into injuries. This is especially useful when starting a new sport, returning after time off or after a previous injury.

What to do if you get injured

For a new sprain or strain, protect the area for the first day or two, then start gentle movement as pain allows. Light activity usually helps healing more than complete rest. Seek urgent care if you can't put weight on the leg, there's an obvious deformity, you hear a pop with immediate swelling, or you hit your head and have concussion symptoms. Our guide on when to see a physiotherapist after an injury explains how to decide.

Frequently asked questions

Should I stretch before playing sports?

A dynamic warm-up is better before sport than long static stretches. Save longer static stretching for after activity or a separate session.

Can a physiotherapist help prevent sports injuries?

Yes. A physiotherapist can assess your strength, mobility and movement patterns, identify risk factors and give you a targeted warm-up and strength program.

How soon can I return to sport after a sprain?

It depends on how severe it is. Mild ankle sprains may take 1 to 3 weeks, and more severe sprains can take 6 weeks or longer. Returning based on strength and balance tests lowers the risk of re-injury.

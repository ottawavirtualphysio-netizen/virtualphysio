Lower Back Pain: What to Do at Home and When to See a Physio

Most people will have lower back pain at some point. The good news: most episodes improve within a few weeks, and the right early steps can make recovery faster and less frightening.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished November 25, 20263 min read

Start with the facts

  • Most lower back pain is not caused by serious damage. Back pain that isn't linked to a specific serious cause is very common, and the outlook is usually good.
  • Pain intensity doesn't equal damage. Back muscles and joints can be very sensitive, especially during a flare-up.
  • Scans often show "wear and tear" in people without any pain. They're rarely needed in the first weeks unless there are red flags.
  • Staying active is better than bed rest for most people.

Red flags: when to get urgent care

Go to the emergency department if back pain comes with: numbness in the groin or inner thighs; new difficulty controlling your bladder or bowel; weakness in your legs that is getting worse; fever or feeling very unwell; or pain after a significant fall or accident. See your doctor soon if you have a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is constant and worse at night regardless of position.

What to do in a flare-up

  1. Keep moving, gently. Short walks several times a day are one of the best things you can do.
  2. Change positions often. Avoid sitting or lying in one position for long periods.
  3. Use heat. A heating pad or warm shower can ease muscle spasm.
  4. Find comfortable rest positions. Try lying on your back with a pillow under your knees, or on your side with a pillow between your knees.
  5. Pace activities. Do a little of what you need to do and take breaks, rather than pushing through or stopping completely.
  6. Ask about medication. A pharmacist or doctor can advise what's safe for you.

Six gentle exercises to try

Move within a comfortable range. Mild discomfort is okay; sharp or increasing pain is a sign to ease off.

  1. Knee rocks. Lie on your back with knees bent and gently let both knees sway side to side. 10 to 15 times.
  2. Knee to chest. Gently hug one knee toward your chest, then the other. Hold 15 to 20 seconds each.
  3. Pelvic tilts. Lying on your back, gently flatten and then arch your lower back. 10 slow repetitions.
  4. Cat–cow. On hands and knees, slowly round then gently arch your back. 10 repetitions.
  5. Bridges. Lying on your back with knees bent, lift your hips a comfortable height. 8 to 10 repetitions.
  6. Walking. Start with 5 to 10 minutes and build up gradually.

Common back pain myths

MythWhat the evidence says
"I should rest until it stops hurting."Staying active usually leads to faster recovery than bed rest.
"Bending and lifting are dangerous."Backs are strong and designed to bend. Building up to lifting is part of recovery.
"I need an MRI to know what's wrong."Imaging is rarely needed early and often finds changes unrelated to pain.
"My disc slipped out of place."Discs don't "slip". Even disc bulges often settle over time.

How physiotherapy helps back pain

A physiotherapist will check for red flags, assess how you move and what aggravates and eases your pain, and explain what's going on. Treatment typically includes a graded exercise program, hands-on techniques for short-term relief, advice on sleep, work and lifting, and a plan to prevent future flare-ups. Exercise therapy is one of the most consistently recommended treatments for persistent low back pain.

When to book

  • Pain isn't clearly improving after one to two weeks
  • Pain or tingling spreads down one leg
  • Your back keeps "going out"
  • You're avoiding work, exercise or activities you enjoy because of fear of making it worse

Frequently asked questions

How long does lower back pain usually last?

Many acute episodes improve significantly within 2 to 6 weeks. If pain lasts longer than 12 weeks, it's considered persistent, and a structured physiotherapy program is especially helpful.

Is walking good for lower back pain?

Yes. Walking is one of the most helpful activities during a flare-up. Start with short, comfortable walks and build up gradually.

Should I see a physiotherapist or a doctor for back pain?

If you have any red-flag symptoms, see a doctor or go to the emergency department. Otherwise, you can book directly with a physiotherapist in Ontario, and they'll refer you if needed.

This article is general information and isn't a substitute for an individual assessment. If you're unsure about your symptoms, speak to a physiotherapist or your doctor.

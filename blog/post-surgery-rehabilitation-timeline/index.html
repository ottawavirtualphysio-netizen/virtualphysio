Post-Surgery Rehabilitation Timeline: What to Expect Week by Week

Recovery after surgery doesn't happen all at once. It moves through phases, and knowing what each phase is for helps you push when it's safe and rest when it's needed.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished July 10, 2026 · Updated November 25, 20263 min read
Every surgery and every surgeon's protocol is different. Always follow the specific restrictions your surgical team gives you. The timeline below is a general guide to common orthopaedic procedures such as knee or hip replacement, ACL reconstruction, rotator cuff repair and fracture fixation.

Why rehab matters after surgery

Surgery repairs or replaces a structure, but it doesn't restore the strength, range of motion and confidence you lost before and after the operation. Muscles weaken quickly when they aren't used, and joints stiffen when they aren't moved. A structured rehab plan rebuilds those things in the right order, so the repair is protected while it heals.

Phase 1: Protection and early movement (roughly weeks 0–2)

The goals are to control pain and swelling, protect the surgical site and start gentle movement.

  • Managing swelling with elevation, ice and compression as advised
  • Gentle range-of-motion exercises within your surgeon's limits
  • Muscle activation exercises, such as tightening the thigh muscle after knee surgery
  • Safe walking with crutches, a walker or a cane, and practice getting in and out of bed, chairs, the car and the shower
  • Breathing exercises and frequent short walks to support circulation

Many people find in-home or virtual sessions especially helpful in this phase, because travelling to a clinic is hard.

Phase 2: Restoring motion and control (roughly weeks 2–6)

As the incision heals and pain settles, the focus moves to getting back full, comfortable range of motion and normal movement patterns.

  • Progressing stretches and range-of-motion work
  • Weaning off walking aids when your walking pattern is safe
  • Low-load strengthening such as bridges, step-ups or band exercises
  • Balance work, which is often affected after lower-limb surgery

Phase 3: Strength and function (roughly weeks 6–12)

This is where most of the visible progress happens. Exercises become more challenging and more specific to your daily life.

  • Progressive strengthening with more resistance or more repetitions
  • Functional training: stairs, carrying, getting up from the floor, reaching overhead
  • Building endurance with walking or cycling
  • Planning a return to work and driving, based on your surgeon's advice

Phase 4: Return to full activity (3 months and beyond)

For many procedures, the final phase prepares you for sport, heavier work or recreational activities. It can last several months. After an ACL reconstruction, for example, return to sport is often planned for nine months or later and is based on strength and movement testing, not just time.

Typical timelines by procedure

Total knee replacement~2–6 weeks~6–12 weeks6–12 months
Total hip replacement~2–4 weeks~6–8 weeks6–12 months
ACL reconstruction~2–4 weeks~6–12 weeks9–12 months for sport
Rotator cuff repairNot applicable~3–4 months6–12 months

These are broad ranges. Your age, health before surgery, the type of procedure and how consistently you do your exercises all affect your own timeline.

Warning signs to report right away

Contact your surgeon, call 911 or go to the emergency department if you notice: calf pain, swelling or warmth in one leg; sudden shortness of breath or chest pain; fever or chills; redness, warmth or discharge from the incision; or a sudden large increase in pain or loss of movement.

Tips for a smoother recovery

  • Start before surgery if you can. "Prehab" builds strength and teaches you your exercises in advance.
  • Little and often beats a lot occasionally. Short, frequent exercise sessions are usually better than one long session.
  • Use pain as a guide. Mild discomfort that settles within a few hours is usually acceptable. Pain that keeps increasing is a sign to adjust.
  • Sleep, eat well and stay hydrated. Tissue healing depends on it.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start physiotherapy after surgery?

Often within days of surgery, but follow your surgeon's instructions. Many people start with gentle movement in hospital and continue with outpatient, in-home or virtual physiotherapy after discharge.

Can post-surgery rehab be done virtually?

Much of it can. Exercise progression, education and monitoring work well online. Hands-on treatment and in-person checks of wound healing or walking aids may need an in-home visit.

How many physiotherapy sessions will I need after surgery?

It varies by procedure. Many people attend weekly or twice weekly for 6 to 12 weeks, then less often as they become independent with their program.

This article is general information and isn't a substitute for an individual assessment. If you're unsure about your symptoms, speak to a physiotherapist or your doctor.

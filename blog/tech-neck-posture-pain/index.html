Tech Neck: How Physiotherapy Helps Neck and Posture Pain from Desk Work

Long days at a laptop, evenings on a phone and not much movement in between: it's no surprise neck and upper-back pain are some of the most common reasons people see a physiotherapist.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished July 10, 2026 · Updated November 25, 20263 min read

What is "tech neck"?

"Tech neck" is an informal term for neck, shoulder and upper-back pain linked to long periods looking down at screens. It isn't a medical diagnosis. It's a pattern: sustained postures, few movement breaks and muscles that are asked to hold the same position for hours.

Posture itself isn't "bad". Research doesn't show one perfect posture that prevents pain. The bigger issue is staying in any position for too long. Your best posture is your next one.

Common symptoms

  • Aching or stiffness in the neck and between the shoulder blades
  • Tight, tender muscles across the top of the shoulders
  • Headaches that start at the base of the skull and spread forward
  • Reduced ability to turn your head, for example when shoulder-checking while driving
  • Pain that builds through the workday and eases on weekends

Five desk-friendly exercises

Try these a few times a day. They should feel easy to moderate, not painful.

  1. Chin tucks. Sit tall and gently glide your chin straight back, as if making a double chin. Hold 3 seconds and repeat 10 times.
  2. Shoulder blade squeezes. Draw your shoulder blades back and slightly down. Hold 5 seconds and repeat 10 times.
  3. Upper-back extension. Hands behind your head, gently arch your upper back over the top of your chair. Repeat 8 to 10 times.
  4. Neck rotations. Slowly turn to look over one shoulder, then the other. Repeat 5 times each side.
  5. Doorway chest stretch. Forearms on a door frame, step through gently until you feel a stretch across your chest. Hold 20 to 30 seconds.

Strengthening also matters. Exercises for the deep neck muscles and the muscles around the shoulder blades, such as rows and band pull-aparts, have good evidence for long-term neck pain.

Workstation set-up tips

  • Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away
  • For laptops, use a stand with a separate keyboard and mouse
  • Elbows close to your sides and roughly at desk height
  • Feet supported on the floor or a footrest
  • Phone raised toward eye level instead of looking down for long stretches

Build in movement breaks

A 1 to 2 minute break every 30 to 45 minutes does more than any chair. Stand up, walk to get water, roll your shoulders or do two of the exercises above. Setting a reminder on your computer helps.

How physiotherapy helps

A physiotherapist will assess your neck and upper-back movement, strength and nerve function, and look at your work habits. Treatment may include hands-on techniques to ease stiffness, a progressive strengthening program, workstation advice and strategies for headaches. Because so much of the treatment is education and exercise, virtual physiotherapy works very well for desk-related neck pain.

When to book an assessment

Book an assessment if neck pain lasts more than a week or two, keeps coming back, disturbs your sleep or causes headaches. See a doctor urgently if neck pain comes with numbness or weakness in your arms, problems with balance or walking, a severe sudden headache, fever, or follows a fall or car accident.

Frequently asked questions

Can bad posture cause neck pain?

Staying in any one posture for a long time can lead to neck pain. Changing position often and staying strong matters more than holding a single perfect posture.

How long does tech neck take to improve?

Many people notice improvement within 2 to 6 weeks once they change their habits and do their exercises consistently. Long-standing pain can take longer.

Is virtual physiotherapy effective for neck pain?

Yes. Assessment, exercise, education and workstation advice can all be done well over video, and you can show your physiotherapist your actual desk set-up.

This article is general information and isn't a substitute for an individual assessment. If you're unsure about your symptoms, speak to a physiotherapist or your doctor.

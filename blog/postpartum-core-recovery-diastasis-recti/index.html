Postpartum Core Recovery and Diastasis Recti: A Physiotherapy Guide

Your body did something remarkable. Rebuilding your core after pregnancy takes time, and it works best when you start gently and progress at the right pace for you.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished November 25, 20263 min read

What is diastasis recti?

Diastasis recti abdominis (DRA) is a widening of the gap between the two sides of the "six-pack" muscle, along a band of connective tissue called the linea alba. It happens naturally during pregnancy to make room for your growing baby, and it's present in most people by the third trimester.

For many people the gap narrows on its own in the first weeks to months after birth. For others it persists, sometimes with a doming or bulging along the middle of the tummy when getting up or lifting.

A gap by itself isn't "bad". What matters most is how well the tissue and surrounding muscles manage pressure and load during everyday tasks.

How to check for diastasis recti

  1. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat.
  2. Place your fingertips horizontally on the midline just above your belly button.
  3. Gently lift your head and shoulders a little off the floor.
  4. Feel how many finger-widths fit in the gap, and how firm or soft the tissue feels.
  5. Repeat above and below the belly button.

A self-check gives you a rough idea. A pelvic health physiotherapist can assess the width, depth and tension of the tissue and, more importantly, how your core functions as a whole.

Gentle exercises in the early weeks

If your birth was uncomplicated and your care provider agrees, gentle exercises can often start within days. After a caesarean birth, follow your surgical team's guidance and be gentler around the incision.

  • Connection breathing. Breathe in and let your ribs and belly expand. As you breathe out, gently draw up through the pelvic floor and in through the lower tummy.
  • Pelvic floor activation. Gentle squeeze-and-lift, then fully relax. Start with a few repetitions a few times a day.
  • Heel slides. Lying on your back, slowly slide one heel away and back while keeping your tummy calm.
  • Pelvic tilts and glute bridges as you feel ready.
  • Walking, gradually increasing your distance.

What to modify early on

  • Roll onto your side to get out of bed instead of sitting straight up.
  • Watch for doming along your midline during exercise. If you see it, make the exercise easier.
  • Breathe out during effort, like lifting the car seat, and avoid holding your breath.
  • Hold off on full crunches, sit-ups and planks until you can control pressure well.
  • Avoid running and jumping until you've built a strength foundation. Return-to-running guidelines often suggest waiting at least 12 weeks after birth and passing some basic strength checks.

Progressing your core

Once the basics feel easy, a physiotherapist can help you add load gradually: side-lying and four-point exercises, then planks and loaded carries, then running and lifting. The goal isn't to avoid all abdominal work. It's to build up so your core handles life with a baby and the activities you love.

When to see a pelvic health physiotherapist

  • Leaking urine, wind or stool
  • Heaviness or bulging in the vagina
  • Pain with sex after you've been cleared to resume
  • A gap or doming that isn't improving after 8 to 12 weeks
  • Back or pelvic pain
  • You want a safe plan to return to running, lifting or sport

Many postpartum appointments can be done virtually, with baby in the room. Learn more about pelvic floor physiotherapy.

Frequently asked questions

Does diastasis recti go away on its own?

For many people the gap narrows naturally in the first 8 weeks after birth and continues to improve over the following months. Targeted exercise can help if it doesn't.

When can I start exercising after giving birth?

Gentle breathing, pelvic floor exercises and walking can often begin within days after an uncomplicated birth. Check with your care provider, especially after a caesarean.

Can I do crunches with diastasis recti?

Not right away. Many people can reintroduce crunch-type exercises later once they can control pressure and doming, ideally with guidance from a physiotherapist.

This article is general information and isn't a substitute for an individual assessment. If you're unsure about your symptoms, speak to a physiotherapist or your doctor.

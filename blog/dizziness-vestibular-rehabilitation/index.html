Dizziness and Balance Problems: How Vestibular Rehabilitation Helps

Dizziness can make everyday life feel unsteady, from rolling over in bed to walking through a busy store. Many causes are very treatable with the right exercises.

By the Virtual Physio teamPublished November 25, 20263 min read
Call 911 right away if dizziness comes on suddenly with any of these: face drooping, arm or leg weakness, slurred speech, double vision, a sudden severe headache, chest pain or fainting. These can be signs of a stroke or heart problem.

What is the vestibular system?

Your vestibular system, in the inner ear, works with your eyes and the sensors in your muscles and joints to tell your brain where your head is in space. When one part sends mixed signals, the result can be vertigo (a spinning sensation), light-headedness, unsteadiness or blurred vision with head movement.

Common causes physiotherapy can help

  • BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo). Brief, intense spinning triggered by rolling over in bed, looking up or bending down. It's caused by tiny crystals in the inner ear moving into the wrong canal. It's one of the most common causes of vertigo, especially in older adults.
  • Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitis. Inflammation of the inner ear or its nerve, often after a viral illness, causing days of vertigo followed by ongoing unsteadiness.
  • Concussion. Dizziness, visual sensitivity and balance problems are common after a head injury.
  • Age-related balance decline. Reduced strength, sensation and reaction time increase the risk of falls.
  • Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD). Ongoing unsteadiness that's often worse in busy visual environments.

How vestibular rehabilitation works

A physiotherapist will ask detailed questions about your dizziness, then test eye movements, balance, walking and, if appropriate, positional tests for BPPV. Treatment is tailored to the cause:

  • Repositioning manoeuvres, such as the Epley manoeuvre for BPPV, guide the displaced crystals back to where they belong. Research reviews have found the Epley manoeuvre is effective for posterior-canal BPPV in the short term (see references).
  • Gaze stabilization exercises train your eyes and inner ear to work together during head movement.
  • Habituation exercises gradually reduce sensitivity to movements or environments that trigger dizziness.
  • Balance and walking training builds steadiness and confidence.
  • Strength training for the legs and hips supports balance and reduces fall risk.

Balance safety at home

  • Remove loose rugs and clutter from walkways.
  • Use night lights between the bedroom and bathroom.
  • Install grab bars in the shower and beside the toilet.
  • Use the handrail on stairs, every time.
  • Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing up.
  • Wear supportive, closed footwear indoors.

An in-home physiotherapy visit is a great way to check your home for fall hazards and practise balance where you actually live.

Can vestibular rehab be done virtually?

Many parts can, including gaze stabilization, habituation and balance exercises, as long as you have a safe set-up and someone nearby if needed. Positional testing and repositioning for BPPV are usually best done in person.

Frequently asked questions

Can physiotherapy help vertigo?

For BPPV, a common cause of vertigo, research supports repositioning manoeuvres such as the Epley. Other causes of dizziness may be helped by targeted exercise. A physiotherapist will assess which applies to you.

What does a vestibular physiotherapist do?

They assess the cause of your dizziness or imbalance and treat it with repositioning techniques, eye-head coordination exercises, habituation and balance training.

When should dizziness be checked by a doctor?

Seek urgent care for sudden dizziness with stroke-like symptoms, chest pain or fainting. See a doctor if dizziness is new, frequent, or comes with hearing loss or ringing in the ears.
